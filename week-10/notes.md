## 3. Event, Weakness, Consequence and Risk

Use your OWN non-clinic example to separate these four ideas.

```text
My example setting: A bakery using a cash register system.
Threat / event (what could happen): Someone could steal an employee's login information.
Vulnerability (the weakness that lets it cause harm): Employees share one password that's written near the register.
Consequence (what goes wrong if it happens): Someone could access sales information or change transactions.
Risk (how the pieces combine into something to manage): The shared password makes unauthorized access more likely, which could cost the bakery money.
```

## 4. Observed, Inferred and Unknown

Evidence you saw is different from a guess. If you did not see a safeguard, it is unknown — not proof it is missing.

```text
Something I observed directly: I saw the register password written on a sticky note.
Something I inferred from it: Someone who sees the note might be able to use the register account.
Something that is still unknown: Whether anyone has used the password without permission.
How I will label a safeguard I did not see: Unknown
```

## 5. Warning Signs and Safe Reporting

Suspicious is not the same as proven.

```text
Warning signs I would look for: Strange email addresses, urgent messages, suspicious links, and requests for passwords.
How I would safely verify without clicking or replying: Contact the company using a phone number or website I already know is legitimate.
Who I would report to, and how: Tell my manager or IT support using the normal reporting process.
Why suspicion alone does not prove compromise: A suspicious message doesn't mean someone clicked it or that an account was hacked.
```

## 6. Likelihood and Impact

Likelihood and impact are each rated 1–3. The classroom score is Likelihood x Impact. Bands: 1–2 low, 3–4 medium, 6–9 high. These are classroom judgments, not measured probabilities.

```text
What a likelihood of 1, 2 or 3 means to me: 1 = Unlikely, 2 = Possible, 3 = More likely because the weakness is regularly exposed.
What an impact of 1, 2 or 3 means to me: 1 = Small problem, 2 = Noticeable disruption, 3 = Serious damage or shutdown.
Existing controls vs proposed controls, in my words: Existing controls are protections already being used. Proposed controls are protections I recommend adding.
Why recording my reason matters more than the number: The number is my judgment, but the explanation shows what evidence I used and why I chose it.
```

## 7. Controls and Residual Risk

Use a non-clinic example.

```text
A specific control: Give each bakery employee their own register login.
How it helps: It makes it easier to track activity and remove access when someone leaves.
Risk remaining afterwards: Someone could still steal an employee's password or misuse their account.
```
